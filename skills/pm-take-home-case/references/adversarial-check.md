# The adversarial check

Run this after the framing memo exists and before the deliverable gets built.
The framing is cheap to change at this point and expensive to change later.

The purpose is not to generate objections for their own sake. It is that a case
study written from only the candidate's seat reads like a pitch, and one that has
survived three hostile readings reads like judgment. The findings are also the
most quotable material in the final artifact: the strongest value propositions
usually surface here, not in the original framing.

## How to run a seat

Take the seat fully. State who you are, what you are accountable for, and what
would make you say no. Then read the framing memo and attack it. Write the
findings down as the persona, in their language, not as a summary of concerns.

Three or four findings per seat is the right depth. More than that and they stop
being sharp.

---

## Seat 1: The buyer

Pick the specific person who signs. Not a segment. A partner at a 150-person
firm, a VP of engineering at a 400-person company, a clinic operations director.
Give them a real situation: what their week looks like, what they are measured
on, what they have been burned by.

Their killer question is usually **liability or credibility**, not price.

What this seat reliably surfaces:

- **A better value proposition than the one you wrote.** The buyer's real reason
  to care is almost never the one in the framing memo. It is more selfish and
  more concrete. In one case the memo said "faster onboarding" while the buyer's seat said
  "the receipts when a client re-opens a settled question." The second became
  the headline.
- **The adoption landmine.** Something operationally or legally uncomfortable
  that the framing quietly assumed away. Recording meetings, touching customer
  data, changing who approves what.
- **What they will not believe.** Usually anything demonstrated on sample data.
  This tends to force a concierge or pilot phase into the roadmap, which
  strengthens it.
- **The status cost.** Products that remove someone's expertise from the loop
  also remove their standing. Being the person everyone asks is worth something.
  Sell the reduction, not the elimination.

---

## Seat 2: The founder or hiring manager

This is the person who will actually read the submission. They are accountable
for the company surviving, and their question is **why this, why us, why now,
and what does it cost us in focus**.

What this seat reliably surfaces:

- **The focus objection.** A small company building a second product has to
  answer it. The answer is never enthusiasm. It is cheap early phases, shared
  infrastructure, hard kill criteria, and a reason the new work improves the
  existing product.
- **The platform story.** Founders think about whether their current product
  gets commoditized. If the proposal turns a point tool into something with
  compounding value, say so in those terms.
- **The window.** How long before a large incumbent notices. Naming a specific
  window and the defense inside it beats claiming a moat.
- **Kill and scale criteria.** What would make them stop. Specific criteria are
  the single most credible thing a founding PM can put in a case study, because
  they show the candidate is accountable rather than promotional.

---

## Seat 3: The evaluator

The reader grading against a rubric, who has read many of these. Their question
is **what would make me rank this below another candidate**.

Check for:

- **Unsourced or soft numbers.** They said they would check. Any figure without
  a primary source is a liability, not a benefit.
- **A missing required section.** Walk the prompt's list literally.
- **Scope that was never cut.** A case study that includes everything shows no
  judgment. The cut list is graded as heavily as the build list.
- **No stated behavior change.** If nobody has to do anything differently, the
  product is not real. If someone does, whose incentive covers it?
- **The obvious answer, unnamed.** If the deliverable never acknowledges the
  framing most candidates would pick, the evaluator assumes it was not
  considered.
- **A pitch with no counter-signal.** Real markets have contradicting evidence.
  Find it, include it, explain why the argument survives.
- **Numbers in prose instead of a table**, and slides that are topic labels
  rather than assertions. Presentation is part of the grade.

---

## What to do with the findings

Fold each into the deliverable where it belongs:

| Finding type | Where it lands |
|---|---|
| Better value proposition | Personas, the pitch headline |
| Adoption landmine | Risk table, with the mitigation |
| Credibility requirement | Versioning, usually as a pilot phase |
| Focus objection | Risk table and the phasing logic |
| Window and defense | Why now, or competitive |
| Kill and scale criteria | Versioning, and the closing slide |
| Missing section or soft number | Fix it directly |

Then tell the user what the pressure test changed. It is useful walkthrough
material: "here is what I believed at first and what changed my mind" is a
strong answer to almost any interviewer question about trade-offs.

## The honesty rule

Simulated critique is reasoning, not evidence. It never gets presented as user
research, customer interviews, or validation. In the deliverable, findings from
this step appear as reasoning or as hypotheses to test, and the investigations
section says what real access would be used to check them. An evaluator who
catches a simulated interview presented as a real one will discount everything
else in the submission.
