# Source discipline

Case prompts increasingly say some version of "any figure must be verified and
cited, we will check." Take that literally. An unsourced number is worse than no
number, and a fabricated one ends the candidacy. The upside is that visible
sourcing rigor is rare enough to be a differentiator on its own.

## The ledger

One file, in its own folder, separate from the drafts. Every number that will
appear anywhere in the deliverable gets a row before it gets used:

| Fact | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| The claim, as it will appear | Link to the primary source | How to use it, caveats, what it implies |

The notes column earns its place. It holds the reasoning: whether a figure is
regional, how old it is, what it implies for the argument, and whether it needs
labeling.

Give each row a short reference ID (M1, T3, C7) and carry those IDs into the
deliverable in brackets. The appendix maps them back. This makes a reviewer's
check trivial, which is the point.

## What counts as credible

Strongest to weakest:

1. **The primary organization's own publication.** Industry association surveys,
   the company's own press release, a regulator, a government statistics agency.
2. **A named research firm's study**, cited with its edition and sample size.
   Sample size is worth including when you have it.
3. **Trade press reporting on a primary source**, when the primary is paywalled
   or will not parse. Cite the trade outlet and name the underlying report.
4. **The company's own claims about itself**, labeled as such. Customer lists and
   user counts are marketing. Using them is fine; presenting them as independent
   is not.

Not credible, no matter how widely repeated: SEO content farms, "statistics
roundup" pages, AI-generated listicles, and blog posts citing other blog posts.

## Handling the number that does not exist

Two failure modes here. One is inventing a figure. The other is quietly dropping
the argument that needed it. Both are avoidable.

**Give the formula with the audience's own inputs, plus one sourced anchor.**
An evaluator can run it against their own business, which is more persuasive than
a figure they would have to trust. It also turns the gap into a demonstration of
rigor rather than a hole.

Then say plainly that the number does not exist publicly and name it as something
the first phase of the plan measures. A case study that says "no public data
exists on this, so V0 measures it" reads as more credible than one that produces
a suspiciously precise figure.

**Adjacent-industry data can stand in, labeled.** A construction-side figure used
in an architecture argument is directional evidence, not proof. Say which it is.
Same for data from another country: label it.

## The do-not-use list

Keep it in the ledger. When a number appears in many search results but traces
back only to content farms, write the row anyway with a "do not use" marker and
name the bad source.

This does real work. These numbers are sticky, they surface again on a later
search, and without the note a future pass may quietly adopt one. In one case a
set of plausible turnover and time-to-hire figures circulated across dozens of
pages and traced back only to a single SEO listicle. The row kept them out.

## Counter-signals

Search for evidence against the thesis, not just for it, and put the strongest
one in the deliverable with a sentence on why the argument survives.

This is not fairness for its own sake. An evaluator who knows the market will
think of the counter-signal themselves. If the submission already named it, the
submission is credible. If not, the whole thing reads as a pitch that did not
look hard.

The strongest form is specific: name the contrary finding, cite it, and show the
argument does not depend on the thing it contradicts.
